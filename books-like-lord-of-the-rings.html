10 Fantasy Series Like Lord of the Rings

A sprawling epic about magic, prophecy, and the eternal battle between good and evil. 14 books of incredible world-building.

📅 April 7, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read

Why Tolkien Fans Keep Searching for More

J.R.R. Tolkien built something that has never truly been replicated: a world with its own languages, histories, cosmologies, and a sense that the myths he invented were real. The Lord of the Rings is not just a story about a ring — it is the feeling of being handed a map of somewhere that actually exists. When readers finish The Return of the King and close the book on that final page, the withdrawal is real. The world feels smaller, the stakes feel lower, and no other fantasy seems to carry the same weight.

The good news is that the decades since Tolkien published have produced an extraordinary body of epic fantasy that takes his legacy in genuinely different directions. Some authors consciously emulate his style — the lush prose, the mythic register, the sense of ancient history pressing on the present. Others deliberately subvert or depart from it, asking what epic fantasy looks like when the prophecy is wrong, when the hero fails, or when the quest itself is revealed to be a tool of oppression. This list covers the full range: both the Tolkien-like experiences that will satisfy that immediate post-Middle-earth hunger, and the series that take the epic fantasy form and push it somewhere new.

How We Evaluate Epic Fantasy

When recommending series to Tolkien fans, NovelPick scores each entry across four dimensions:

  • World-building depth: Does the world feel historically layered, geologically real, and culturally coherent?
  • Narrative scope: Does the story earn its epic ambitions, or does scale serve as a substitute for substance?
  • Character complexity: Are the protagonists more than vessels for the plot? Do they change, fail, and grow?
  • Prose quality: Does the writing match the ambition of the world?

What to Look For

Tolkien fans typically share a taste for worlds that feel inhabited rather than constructed — histories that bleed into the present, magic systems rooted in mythology rather than game mechanics, and stakes that are existential without being nihilistic. The series on this list all share at least three of those four qualities.

1. The Wheel of Time

by Robert Jordan · Tor · 1990–2013 · 14 books · 4.4 million words

The Wheel of Time is the closest thing English-language fantasy has to a direct heir of Tolkien's legacy — and it is also one of the most controversial series in the genre. Robert Jordan took everything that made The Lord of the Rings monumental — the world-spanning quest, the ancient prophecy, the dark lord, the fellowship of unlikely heroes — and expanded it into a fourteen-book, four-million-word meditation on power, gender, fate, and what it means to be the hero of your own story when prophecy says you have no choice.

The world of Rand al'Thor is not Middle-earth, but it shares the same fundamental appeal: a world whose history is real, whose magic has rules, and where every location feels like it has been lived in before the story began. The One Power — divided into male saidin and female saidar — creates a gender metaphysics that Jordan takes seriously as a philosophical problem, not just a narrative mechanic. The Aes Sedai, the White Tower, the Forsaken, the Blight: all of it coheres into something that rewards both casual reading and obsessive annotation.

Where it differs from Tolkien: The Wheel of Time is considerably longer, more romance-heavy in its middle books, and Jordan's treatment of gender is more philosophically explicit. It also has a far more optimistic view of whether the hero can actually win. The final book, A Memory of Light, was finished by Brandon Sanderson after Jordan's death in 2007 — Sanderson's voice is subtly different from Jordan's, and readers notice.

Best for: Readers who want the longest, most immersive journey possible, and who do not mind a series that takes its time with character relationships before delivering its most devastating payoffs.

2. The Malazan Book of the Fallen

by Steven Erikson · Tor · 1999–2011 · 10 books · 3.3 million words

Where The Wheel of Time is Tolkien's direct successor, The Malazan Book of the Fallen is Tolkien's philosophical antagonist. Steven Erikson — a trained archaeologist and anthropologist — looked at the epic fantasy tradition and asked: what if the gods are real, the magic is brutal, and the ancient evil is just another empire that got too powerful? The result is a ten-book deconstruction of the genre that somehow also delivers some of the most emotionally devastating fantasy ever written.

The Malazan world has a paleontological depth — civilizations rise and fall, gods are created by belief, and the history of the world is not a linear story but a stratigraphy of collapsed empires. Erikson throws the reader in at the deep end without exposition or hand-holding; the first hundred pages of Gardens of the Moon are famously disorienting. But those who persist find one of the most rewarding series in the genre, particularly for readers who want fantasy that treats philosophy, colonialism, and trauma with the same seriousness as sword fights.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Malazan is deliberately anti-Tolkien in many ways. There is no chosen one — there are soldiers, mages, and gods doing their best in an incomprehensibly large world. The prose is more sardonic, the violence is more graphic, and the philosophical ambition is considerably higher. It is also far harder to read: Erikson assumes an intelligent reader who will put things together rather than having them explained.

Best for: Readers who want something even more intricate and intellectually ambitious than Tolkien, and who do not mind being confused for the first book. The payoff is proportional to the investment.

3. The Stormlight Archive

by Brandon Sanderson · Tor · 2010–ongoing · 5 books published (of 10 planned) · 2.1 million words

Brandon Sanderson built his reputation on his "hard magic systems" — fantasy where the rules of magic are as rigorously defined as science, and where problem-solving within those rules creates some of the most satisfying action sequences in modern fantasy. The Stormlight Archive is his magnum opus: a ten-book series set on the storm-scoured world of Roshar, where civilization clings to existence between catastrophic supernatural storms that have shaped not just the geography but the biology, architecture, and psychology of every civilization on the planet.

The magic system — centered on Stormlight, the Nahel bond between humans and spren, and the ten Radiant orders — is the most elaborately constructed Sanderson has ever attempted, and it rewards close reading in ways that most epic fantasy does not. But the series' real power lies in its characters: Kaladin, a former slave turned bridgeman turned legendary figure; Shallan, a noble daughter hiding a fractured identity; Dalinar, a warlord trying to become something better than what he was. The philosophical through-line — about the nature of honour, obligation, and the cost of power — gives the series a weight that goes well beyond its spectacular magic.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Stormlight has clearer rules, more action-oriented pacing, and a more explicitly structured magic system. The world's aesthetics — the storms, the shell-plate armour, the greatshells — are entirely original. Sanderson's prose is less mythic and more efficient than Tolkien's, but what he sacrifices in atmosphere he gains in pacing.

Best for: Readers who want the most satisfying magic system in modern fantasy, combined with characters who feel like real people growing under impossible pressure. It is the best ongoing series in the genre.

4. The Belgariad + The Malloreon

by David Eddings · 1982–1991 · 5 + 5 books · Complete

David Eddings wrote The Belgariad in deliberate conversation with Tolkien — not as a response or critique, but as a tribute. The result is one of the most beloved and also most criticized series in fantasy: a story that knows exactly what it is, wears its genre conventions openly, and delivers precisely the satisfaction those conventions promise. Garion, a farm boy who discovers he is the chosen one, the ancient evil Godslayer, the Prophecy, the Quest: none of it is subtle, and Eddings does not pretend it is. But the execution is so polished, the pacing so confident, and the character dynamics so genuinely warm that it works.

The Belgariad is comfort reading in the most literal sense — a series designed to make the reader feel the pleasure of familiar patterns being executed at the highest possible level of craft. Eddings' narration is witty, knowing, and frequently breaks the fourth wall in ways that create intimacy rather than breaking immersion. The world is smaller and cozier than Tolkien's — less mythic, more domestic — but that is part of the appeal for readers who want something with less existential weight and more emotional warmth.

Where it differs from Tolkien: The Belgariad is smaller in scope, lighter in tone, and more explicitly playful about its genre conventions. It has a gentle humor that Tolkien's work lacks. The magic system is vague by design — Eddings was not interested in systematizing it — which some readers find refreshing and others find frustrating.

Best for: Readers who want the pure, unironic pleasure of a well-told chosen-one quest without wanting to think too hard about it. It is also an excellent entry point for younger fantasy readers transitioning from YA to adult epic fantasy.

5. The Inheritance Cycle

by Christopher Paolini · Random House · 2003–2011 · 4 books · Complete

Christopher Paolini was seventeen years old when he began writing what would become Eragon, and the Inheritance Cycle is simultaneously one of the most commercially successful and most critically polarizing series in modern fantasy. The story of Eragon — a farm boy who discovers a dragon egg, bonds with the resulting dragon Saphira, and becomes the first Dragon Rider in over a thousand years — draws explicitly and self-consciously from every major fantasy tradition that preceded it, from Tolkien's elves and dwarves to the progression mechanics of xianxia.

The result is a series that is deeply derivative at the level of individual elements but genuinely original in its synthesis. Paolini's world — Alagaesia — is smaller than Tolkien's, but the magic system (the Ancient Language, the energy-based spellcasting) has enough internal logic that it rewards analysis. The series is also notably dark for a book that began as YA: characters die, moral choices have consequences, and the ending is neither simple nor triumphant in the conventional sense.

Where it differs from Tolkien: The Inheritance Cycle is explicitly YA-originated and reads as such in places — the teenage protagonist's perspective, the coming-of-age emotional arcs. The magic system is more systematized than Tolkien's, and the series leans more heavily into romance than most classic epic fantasy.

Best for: Readers who want a series that is easy to read but takes its world seriously, and who are willing to forgive the derivative early setup in exchange for an increasingly original second half.

6. The Chronicles of Thomas Covenant

by Stephen R. Donaldson · 1977–ongoing · 10 books

The Chronicles of Thomas Covenant is the most deliberately challenging entry on this list — and one of the most important. Stephen R. Donaldson wrote the first trilogy, The Unbeliever, as a response to a specific question: what would happen if a modern, traumatized, morally ambiguous man was summoned to a fantasy world and refused to believe any of it was real? Covenant's disbelief is not literary skepticism — it is the psychological defense mechanism of a man with a life-threatening illness who cannot afford to believe in miracles.

The series is famously polarizing. Donaldson writes in a dense, symbolic, almost Jamesian prose register that is as far from the accessible voice of modern fantasy as anything in the genre. The first trilogy in particular is relentlessly dark, with graphic violence, a protagonist who makes choices that are difficult to sympathize with, and a world — The Land — that is genuinely beautiful but increasingly brutal. But for readers who connect with it, the series is a transformative experience. The Land is one of the most poetically rendered worlds in fantasy, and the philosophical questions Donaldson asks about belief, doubt, and what we owe to places that need us — even if we do not believe they exist — are unlike anything else in the genre.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Thomas Covenant is anti-Tolkien in the most fundamental way possible: its protagonist does not accept the quest, doubts the reality of the world he is in, and makes choices that harm people who need him. There is no clear moral compass, no uncomplicated heroism, and no easy answers. The prose style is also far denser and more literary.

Best for: Readers who want fantasy that functions as serious literary fiction, and who are willing to work through a difficult protagonist in exchange for a world and a philosophy that will stay with them for years.

7. The Riftwar Saga

by Raymond E. Feist · 1977–1993 · Multiple arcs · Midkemia universe

Raymond E. Feist's Midkemia universe is one of the most consistently satisfying worlds in epic fantasy — a place where the magic is practical, the politics are personal, and the battles are won by smart people making good decisions under pressure. The Riftwar Saga — covering the war between the Kelewan empire and the Kingdom of the Isles — established the template for many of the political fantasy dynamics that modern epic fantasy relies on: the smart protagonist who is underestimated, the ancient empire with different values, the alliance of convenience between people who do not like each other.

Feist is a working fantasy writer in the best sense: he is not trying to reinvent the genre or make a philosophical statement. He is trying to tell a good story about interesting people in an interesting world, and he does it extremely well. The world-building has real geographic and cultural specificity, the magic system is clear enough to create plot mechanics without being systematized into gamelike predictability, and the characters — particularly the orphaned warrior-scholar Pug — grow across the series in ways that feel earned rather than assigned.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Riftwar is more politically grounded, more military in its central concerns, and more comfortable with moral ambiguity. There are no noble elves, no ancient evils waiting for their moment — the conflicts are driven by human and semi-human political choices. The prose is efficient rather than mythic.

Best for: Readers who want fantasy that is rooted in political and military realism, and who appreciate characters whose growth is the product of practical wisdom rather than mystical destiny.

8. The Shannara Chronicles

by Terry Brooks · 1977–ongoing · 30+ books · Shannara universe

Terry Brooks is the writer who proved that epic fantasy could be a commercial mass-market phenomenon. The Sword of Shannara was the first fantasy novel to appear on the New York Times bestseller list, and Brooks spent the next four decades building one of the most elaborately detailed secondary worlds in the genre. The Shannara universe is post-apocalyptic Earth — the events of our own history have led to the Great Wars that destroyed industrial civilization, and the centuries since have produced a world where magic has returned and the old technologies are remembered as myths.

The world-building is the series' genuine strength: the gradual reveal of how our world became Shannara, the specific flora and fauna that have evolved in response to the magical healing of the planet, the cultural layers of the different races (elves, dwarves, trolls) — all of it coheres into a world that feels geologically real. The narrative structure has more in common with classic adventure fiction than with literary fantasy, and the plotting is clean and propulsive. Brooks is particularly good at the "quest begins in a peaceful place and rapidly becomes a tour of increasingly dangerous terrain" structure that Tolkien perfected.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Shannara is more adventure-oriented, faster-paced, and less philosophically ambitious. The post-apocalyptic framing gives it a specific connection to our world that Tolkien's work deliberately lacks. The magic is more visceral and less mythic in its presentation.

Best for: Readers who want a long, satisfying universe to explore with clear entry points and a wide range of sub-series to choose from. The original Elfstones of Shannara trilogy is the best starting point.

9. The Earthsea Cycle

by Ursula K. Le Guin · 1968–2001 · 5 books · Complete

Ursula K. Le Guin's Earthsea Cycle is the most philosophically serious fantasy series of the twentieth century — and also one of the most quietly beautiful. The world of Earthsea is defined by the balance between the force that gives life and the force that takes it away, and the entire series can be read as a meditation on what it costs to maintain that balance, and who pays the price. Le Guin was a Taoist, an anthropologist, and one of the finest prose stylists American literature has produced, and all three of those dimensions animate every book in the series.

The magic system — True Names, the systemic practice of wizardry, the organization of wizards into a network of islands — is one of the most influential in fantasy, directly influencing everything from Sanderson's work to the magic systems in countless video games. But the real power of Earthsea lies in its treatment of power itself: what it means to have the ability to change things, and what obligation that ability creates. Ged, the protagonist of the first four books, is one of the great characters in fantasy — a man who begins as the most gifted wizard of his generation and spends the rest of his life reckoning with what that gift cost him and others.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Earthsea is smaller in geographic scope, more philosophical in its concerns, and less epic in its narrative ambitions. Le Guin's prose is spare, precise, and deliberate where Tolkien's is expansive and mythic. There is no dark lord, no ancient evil waiting for its moment — the threats in Earthsea are imbalances, and the resolutions are calibrations rather than victories.

Best for: Readers who want fantasy that thinks carefully about what magic costs, and who appreciate a writer who treats her invented world with the same seriousness she treats the real one. A Wizard of Earthsea is one of the best-written fantasy novels in existence.

10. The Farseer Trilogy

by Robin Hobb · 1995–1997 · 3 books + related trilogies · Realm of the Elderlings

Robin Hobb's Realm of the Elderlings is, by common critical consensus, one of the greatest fantasy series ever written — and it begins with a trilogy that is smaller in scope than Tolkien or Jordan but incomparably deeper in emotional precision. The Farseer Trilogy follows FitzChivalry Farseer, the illegitimate son of a prince who is raised in the castle but never quite belongs, trained as a royal assassin but also as a Skill-user (a magical telepathic ability unique to the Farseer bloodline), and forced to navigate the deadly politics of a kingdom that does not quite trust him.

What sets Hobb apart from virtually every other epic fantasy writer is her understanding of psychology. Fitz is not a powerful wizard or a prophesied hero — he is a traumatized young person doing his best in a system that was designed without his interests in mind. The way his relationships form, fracture, and reform across the trilogy is rendered with a precision that makes most fantasy characterization look cartoonish by comparison. Hobb also has a gift for making the fantastical feel physically immediate — the Skill, the Wit magic, and the political conspiracies all have a tactile reality that pulls the reader into the world.

Where it differs from Tolkien: Farseer is far more intimate, more psychologically detailed, and more politically complex than Tolkien. There is no world-shaking quest — the fate of the kingdom rests on Fitz, but his primary struggle is internal: can he become the person he needs to be to protect the people he loves? The prose is more literary and the pacing more deliberate.

Best for: Readers who want fantasy that functions as character-driven literary fiction, and who are willing to invest in a protagonist whose journey is as much internal as external. Once you finish the Farseer Trilogy, the rest of the Realm of the Elderlings (six more trilogies) awaits — it only gets better.

NovelPick's Verdict

New to epic fantasy: Start with The Belgariad — it is the most purely satisfying entry point. Want maximum scope: The Wheel of Time will consume months of your life and leave you devastated in the best way. Want the most intelligent deconstruction: The Malazan Book of the Fallen rewards every ounce of attention you give it. Want the best ongoing series: The Stormlight Archive is unmatched. And if you want to understand what epic fantasy can achieve when it operates at the highest literary level, The Farseer Trilogy by Robin Hobb is simply indispensable.
